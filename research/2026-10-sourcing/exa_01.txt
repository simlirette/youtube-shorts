Title: How to Make Animated Explainer Reels: the @rick.theengineer Formula | Brainrot Shorts Blog
URL: https://www.brainrotshorts.com/blog/how-to-make-animated-explainer-reels
Published: 2026-08-17T11:32:42.000Z
Author: Maiko
Highlights:
A breakdown of the animated explainer reel format behind a 108K-follower account — why word-timed motion graphics hold attention, and how to make your own from a script with AI.
...
Animated explainer reels are short vertical videos that teach one concept with motion graphics — titles, counters, and diagrams cut to every word of a narration. The format built @rick.theengineer to 108K followers with reels approaching a million views, and every one of those videos is generated from a script with the AI Motion Graphics Generator — no animation software involved.

Open @rick.theengineer and scroll the reels tab for a minute. An animated character walks you through how authenticator apps generate codes with no signal. Why the same photo comes out of an export twice the size. What actually happens when an API tells you to slow down. The account sits at 108K followers, and its biggest reels have done 607K, 749K, and 954K views (figures from the public profile, August 2026).
...
First, the format itself — the animated explainer reel — is one of the most reliable performers on Instagram and TikTok right now, and it's almost entirely faceless.

Second, the account isn't hiding how it's made. The bio credits the tool directly: every video comes out of the same AI motion graphics generator you can use today. This isn't a motion designer with a decade of After Effects behind them. It's a script, a narration, and a generator that does the rest.
...
Most short-form video is watched muted, at least at first. Explainer reels are built for that: captions land word by word, timed to the narration, styled big enough to be the visual. A viewer scrolling silently gets the full video; one who unmutes gets narration, music, and sound effects hitting on every cut. Either way, the video works. Footage-first video can't make that promise.
...
Watch any strong explainer reel closely and you'll notice the discipline: at any moment, the screen shows exactly one thing — a stat counting up, a three-step diagram drawing itself, a single sentence stamped in giant type. The viewer never has to choose where to look. That's the core craft of motion design, and it's the thing template editors can't give you, because a template was laid out before your content existed.
...
A static slide gives a viewer permission to leave. Frames that keep resolving — a number still counting, a diagram still connecting, the next caption word lighting up — keep promising something is about to finish, and viewers hold on for completions. It's the same loop that makes progress bars weirdly watchable, run for ninety straight seconds.
...
Rick — the animated character walking through every frame — solves the faceless account's biggest problem: nothing for the audience to attach to. A consistent character does the job of an on-camera personality: instantly recognizable in the feed, no camera, no reshoots. It's optional in the format, but it's a large part of why a feed of tech diagrams feels like a creator rather than a slideshow channel.
...
Animated explainers reward specificity. "How the internet works" is a saturated topic with no natural audience; "why your authenticator app works in airplane mode" makes one specific group of people feel seen. Rick's feed is software engineering, but the format transfers to any field with concepts worth unpacking — finance, medicine, fitness, law, marketing.
...
1. A hook that names a mystery (0–3 seconds). Not "let me explain caching" but "this is why the app feels instant the second time you open it." The hook frames a gap in the viewer's knowledge they suddenly want closed.
2. The setup (3–15 seconds). One or two frames establishing what the viewer already knows — fast, confident, no throat-clearing.
3. The mechanism (15–60 seconds). The heart of the video: the diagram that draws itself, the counter that makes the number visceral, the comparison laid out side by side. This is where the word-timed cutting earns its keep — each element appears at the moment the narration calls for it.
4. The payoff (final 5–10 seconds). One sentence that resolves the hook, usually stamped on screen in big type. The viewer should be able to repeat the answer to a friend.

Sound design runs under all of it: a music bed that stays out ... voice's way and effects that hit when elements land. Viewers rarely notice it consciously; they notice its absence immediately.
...
The old way to produce a minute of this — storyboard, design, keyframe, caption, mix — was a working day for a motion designer or a $300+ invoice. The generator collapses the pipeline into three inputs.
...
- Name numbers. "A JPEG throws away about 90% of the data" becomes a counter and a meter. "JPEGs are much smaller" becomes a shrug.
- Describe steps. "The request hits the cache first, then the database" becomes a diagram that draws itself in order.
- Set up comparisons. "PNG keeps every pixel; JPEG keeps what your eye notices" becomes a split frame.
...
Two options in the generator: pick one of the AI voices to read your script, or upload a recording of yourself narrating. Either way you hear the narration before the video builds, so the voice is never a surprise. Your own voice is worth trying — the animation quality is identical, and audiences bond faster with a real human voice, even on a faceless account.
...
Choose a design style from the library, or have the AI design one for your video: it asks a few questions about audience and mood, then shows you one fully designed frame of your actual content to approve before building the rest. Hit generate, and the AI storyboards the narration, designs and animates every frame, times the captions, and mixes music and sound effects. About twenty minutes later you download a vertical 9:16 MP4, built for Reels, Shorts, and TikTok.
...
The "why is it like this?" reel — pick an everyday annoyance and reveal the mechanism. "This is why your phone's storage fills up even though you deleted everything… " Works because the viewer has personally felt the problem.

The "X vs Y" reel — two things your audience half-understands, one clean verdict. "Supabase and Firebase will both build your app. They'll charge you very differently for it." Works because picking sides is engagement fuel.

The "how it actually works" reel — take something invisible and draw it. "When you tap pay, four companies talk to each other in under two seconds." Works because the diagram is the payoff — the viewer finally sees the thing.
...
- Post a series, not one-offs. Rick's feed works because every video is recognizably from the same universe — same character, same style, same rhythm. Consistency is what turns a viral view into a follow.
- Let the hook be the cover. The first frame should state the mystery in big type; that's what profile visitors tap.
- Cross-post everything. The same vertical MP4 works on Reels, TikTok, and Shorts. Three surfaces, one render.
- Mine your comments. Every "wait, but how does X work?" reply is your next script, pre-validated.
...
Everyone has one — the thing colleagues or friends always ask you to explain. That's your first reel: you already know the confusion, the analogy that lands, and the payoff sentence. Write it down the way you'd say it out loud, hand it to the AI Motion Graphics Generator, and you'll have the animated version rendering before your coffee is done.

Title: Engineering Videos for Instagram and TikTok
URL: https://videodatabase.io/topics/engineering
Published: N/A
Author: N/A
Highlights:
Engineering content on TikTok and Instagram covers everything from mechanical breakdowns to inventor origin stories, making complex technical ideas accessible and genuinely entertaining. If you're looking for engineering video ideas, this is where the format patterns live. The dominant format in engineering content is the vlog-style character profile, and @fdotinc has built a consistent approach around it. The structure is simple but effective: introduce a person, explain the problem they are trying to solve, show the invention or tool, then tell the story of how they got there. What makes this work is that the technical explanation comes wrapped inside a human story. Viewers follow the person first and learn the engineering second. This is why origin story is the most common concept in this topic. Engineers make compelling protagonists because they are motivated by genuine frustration with how things work, and that frustration is relatable even when the technical details are not. Breakdown content is the other major current running through engineering videos. @bixmation takes a different path entirely, using animated avatars to disassemble cars, engines, and performance specs with a kind of enthusiast energy that reads as insider knowledge rather than textbook explanation. The sleeper car format is particularly strong here: take something ordinary-looking, reveal the engineering surprise underneath, then walk through how to exploit that surprise. The Volvo XC90 V8 with its Yamaha-built engine and Noble M600 connection, or the Toyota RAV4 sharing a block with a Lotus supercar, these are the kinds of facts that make engineering content shareable because they reframe something familiar as secretly extraordinary. Process and behind-the-scenes content also performs well in this topic. @filipcustic documents kinetic sculpture work in a way that compresses the full arc of a technical build, from 3D modeling to final installation, into a fast-paced montage that lets the craft speak for itself. This format works because it shows engineering as a creative act, not just a technical one. The final reveal of a working, moving object gives the video a natural payoff that pure explainer content often lacks. Similarly, @craighillcompany uses product design as a teaching moment, explaining the fluid dynamics behind pour spouts while simultaneously introducing a new product. That combination of education and demonstration gives the content a clear purpose that keeps viewers watching. For creators planning engineering content, the clearest opportunity is in making the inventor the story rather than the invention. Audiences engage with the person behind the thing, the moment of frustration that started the project, the technical dead ends, the unexpected collaboration. The engineering itself becomes more interesting when it is framed as someone's obsession rather than a feature list. Whether the format is an animated breakdown, a vlog profile, or a process montage, the videos that work in this topic all share the same underlying move: they find the human stakes inside a technical problem and lead with those.

@craighillcompany builds its entire content strategy around deliberate failure demonstration. Before introducing any solution, it destroys something familiar, cutting open a carabiner, bending a pen clip past its limit, to make the design problem physically undeniable. @fdotinc works from the opposite direction, leading with a person instead of a problem, tracing how a specific founder's obsession produced a specific technical breakthrough. Both approaches trust the viewer with real engineering detail rather than simplifying it away. That trust is what separates engineering content that feels substantive from engineering content that just looks like it.
...
Two hook patterns consistently appear in engineering content and they work through opposite mechanisms. The curiosity open loop, as in "My friend Rank is building a metal reactor in his family's garage," works because the specificity of garage plus metal reactor creates an immediate credibility paradox; the setting undersells the ambition, and the viewer needs to close that gap. The secrets-shortcut hook, as used by @craighillcompany with "When you saw off the catch of a carabiner," works because the destructive act is irreversible; you cannot un-see the interior of a mechanism once someone shows you how to expose it.
...
The videos that perform in this category share one structural commitment: they never let the engineering float in the abstract. Every technical claim is grounded in something a viewer can see, a CAD screen recording mid-cut, a hand holding two versions of the same mechanism, an animated graphic overlaid on actual footage. The format varies from Vlog to Talking Head Edit to Faceless, but the editorial logic stays constant. Complexity earns its place only when it is attached to something tangible. The creators who get this right are not simplifying engineering; they are staging it.
