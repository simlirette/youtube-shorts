## Average View Duration and Swipe-Away Rate

Average view duration is the most important number in Shorts analytics. It reports the average seconds watched before viewers left or the Short ended. To turn it into a percentage, divide it by the total length: a 20-second Short averaging 17 seconds watched is at 85%.

That percentage only means something next to the runtime, so the targets are banded by length. Aim for above 60% on Shorts under 15 seconds, above 50% between 15 and 30 seconds, and above 40% between 30 and 60 seconds. Above 75%, above 65% and above 55% respectively are strong. The retention benchmarks guide sets these out alongside TikTok and Reels.

Swipe-away rate is the inverse signal: the share of viewers who actively swiped on before the Short finished. A high rate concentrated in the first three seconds is a hook problem. A spike at a particular mid-video second identifies the exact moment losing people, which you can go back and watch. Studio overlays this on the retention graph, so you get both where and how badly.

As a working target, keep swipe-away in the first three seconds below 25% on Shorts under 30 seconds, and below 35% on Shorts between 30 and 60 seconds. Treat those as lines to aim at rather than thresholds that unlock anything: no platform publishes a cutoff, and the useful comparison is against your own recent videos of similar length.

## Completion Rate and Swipe-Away Targets

Completion rate is the share of viewers who reach the final second. It measures something different from average view duration: a viewer who watched 95% of a 30-second Short and left just before the end counts as a near-miss on one metric and a failure on the other, and the gap between the two is where ending problems show up.

Aim for above 40% completion on Shorts under 15 seconds, above 30% between 15 and 30 seconds, and above 25% between 30 and 60 seconds. Above 55%, above 45% and above 40% respectively are strong. The Shorts completion rate guide covers what to do when the number falls short.

Swipe-away rate is the inverse signal: the share of viewers who actively moved on before the video finished. Concentrated in the first three seconds it is a hook problem, and keeping it below 25% there is a reasonable target. A spike in the middle is a momentum problem, and it identifies the exact second worth rewatching.

Completion and subscriber conversion move together, because the reason to subscribe usually lives at the end of the video. Improving the ending tends to show up in both numbers at once, which makes it better value than working on either in isolation.
