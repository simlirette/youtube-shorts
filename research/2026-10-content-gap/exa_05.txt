Bohemia Chronicles ran for 33 days and did not miss one single publishing slot. It also earned €0. One month ago I published a forecast before I had any data. I did it on purpose, because I wanted that the numbers get a chance to embarrass me. Well, here we are.

Short version for the busy ones. I was testing eight video formats on one engine over 33 days and map-trace took 76 % of all views. Seamless-journey, which was the format I would bet on, ended fifth.

Instagram gave me 41,799 views and 233 followers, YouTube gave 942 views and 11 subscribers. And the repo took 446 commits in the same window, at least one every single day.

Bohemia Chronicles went live on 3 July. It publishes one 30 to 45 second cinematic history short every weekday, to Instagram and to YouTube Shorts. My work is that I approve storyboards once per week. Everything else after that is job of the pipeline.
...
Between 3 July and morning of 5 August the pipeline published 23 reels to Instagram and 22 Shorts to YouTube. Every weekday slot in this window got filled and I never opened any upload form.
...
YouTube finished on 942 views and 11 subscribers, with 57 likes and 22 comments. Which sounds fine, until you count that it is exactly one comment per video and all of them posted the pipeline itself. Nobody else said anything on that channel yet. It is a bit lonely place, to be honest.

The growth was not linear at all. At day 15 the channel had 4,098 views and 15 followers and I was already writing quite gloomy draft about it. Then the remaining 18 days added the other 37,000 views and I had to throw that draft away.
...
- 45 %: a hobby that pays its own render bills, €20–80 a month.
- 42 %: real side income, €350–1,100 a month.
- 13 %: it takes off properly.

One month in, we are in the 45 % scenario and not even in the paying-its-own-bills half of it. The channel earned nothing so far.

The YouTube Shorts payout route needs 1,000 subscribers and 10 million Shorts views in 90 days. I have 11 subscribers and 942 views. On the views I am four orders of magnitude short, which is a polite way to say very, very far.
...
What landed better than the forecast was the cost side. I budgeted $0.70 to $1.00 per reel for cloud GPU fees, but on the launch day I flipped the default renderer to my home 3060, so most of that money was never spent. The real cost per reel is some electricity plus few cents of image and voice API calls. Billed in dollars, while the forecast and the revenue sit in euros, because why should anything be simple.

One video took 37 % of the entire channel, got 126 shares and average watch time 25.2 seconds. The format itself averages 18.2 seconds, so on this one people stayed noticeably longer than on the maps around it.

It ended fifth and on top of that it costs the most to render. This audience simply did not care how much a scene costs to make.

There is also a good counter-example in the data and I want to keep it visible, not to tell some clean story. On 3 August the pipeline published a map-trace about Cyril and Methodius bringing the Slavic alphabet to Moravia. 245 views. The worst map on the whole channel. It is also the only map without an army on it. Every map with a campaign or a battle cleared 800 views. The one about an alphabet did not.

My working read from this is that movement with stakes wins and a map is the cheapest way I found how to draw it. The next batch will test this instead of assuming it.

My rule from before the launch was that a format needs 30 % of the channel median performance to survive. The median reel does 825 views. On that rule dies only single-subject-loop and quiz-reveal is one bad reel away from dying too.

Production needed me every day, because I kept rebuilding the engine underneath it. The map format alone accounts for most of the last two weeks of work and I built it in this shape because the numbers pointed there.

The channel itself earned €0. The only money in this window came from the product I packaged out of the pipeline, one bundle sale at $69.

So at day 33 what I have is a hobby with unusually good telemetry, sitting more or less exactly where the most likely scenario said it will sit. The forecast got one thing wrong though, and it is the asset. The month produced a working map engine and 22 reels of evidence about what a history audience actually watches. Both are worth more to me than €80 would be.

YouTube introduced the Shorts video format in 2021, allowing users to upload short videos that are prominently displayed on its website and app. Despite having such a large visual footprint, there are no studies to date that have looked at the impact Shorts introduction had on the production and consumption of content on YouTube. This paper presents the first comparative analysis of YouTube Shorts versus regular videos with respect to user engagement (i.e., views, likes, and comments), content creation frequency and video categories. We collected a dataset containing information about 70k channels that posted at least one Short, and we analyzed the metadata of all the videos (9.9M Shorts and 6.9M regular videos) they uploaded between January 2021 and December 2022, spanning a two-year period including the introduction of Shorts. Our longitudinal analysis shows that content creators consistently increased the frequency of Shorts production over this period, especially for newly-created channels, which surpassed that of regular videos. In general, Shorts attract more views and likes per view than regular videos, but attract less comments per view. # 6M Views And 47K Subs From 1 YouTube Short — How I Did It (Case Study)
...
In summer ’24, my AI health education YouTube shorts channel surpassed 115k subscribers in just a few months of starting it.

From that, 47k subs came from a single 6M+ view short, which represents over 40% of the entire subscriber count I had gained (image attached below).

Since that’s an outstanding result, I want to deconstruct the elements of the video which I believe have contributed to its success, so maybe you (a small but ambitious short form creator) can take these principles away and apply them to your own content on social media (whether Shorts, Instagram or TikTok).

## 1: High quality visuals

Since my entire channel was AI based, the images that were used were of high quality and very cinematic. This wasn’t due to the AI software (Midjourney) itself but rather the prompts I gave it, which produced those visuals, which could be a topic for another day.

When all these metrics come together, it signals to the social media algorithm that your content is one of a kind, hence increasing its chances of going viral. It’s one of the best aspects to optimise in your content, and since it’s a frequently neglected aspect, you can easily differentiate yourself if you choose to focus on it.

## 2: Quick transition between each image

In my 6M+ view short, I had quick transitions between each image. Between each frame, there was a short 0.5–1 second gap. Keeping the short very fast paced in this manner was huge because it kept the viewer constantly stimulated every second making it hard for them to disengage.

And the result? An average retention rate of 120.7%! If you don’t incorporate this strategy within your own content, you’re missing out on the opportunity for your video to go viral because attention spans are short, and if you don’t leverage that, viewers will scroll to the video that does. Ideally, you shouldn’t keep an image or clip in your video longer than a couple of seconds as a general rule of thumb.

## 3: Quick sentences

The third element of my viral short, which stood out to me, was the use of quick sentences. This comes down to the script itself. Good scripting is fundamental to your video’s success and because I knew that, I crafted each sentence in a way that would sustain the viewers’ attention.

Each sentence was 2–3 seconds long, and each word in the short was necessary. If a word didn’t need to be included, I left it out. The common trap that so many creators fall into is they focus mainly on their hook and keeping it under 3 seconds. They’re unaware that the principle behind making the opening sentence under 3 seconds applies to the rest of the video so if you waffle afterwards, retention drops fast. Keep what’s necessary and leave everything else out.

## 4: Captivating captions

The fourth aspect of the video that really stood out was the use of compelling captions. This ties back to the visual side of things.

Depending on the font and design of your captions, they can either enhance or downgrade the overall experience of your content, so you must choose wisely. A well chosen font and caption design improves retention because it guides attention more clearly across the screen and reduces the effort needed to follow the content.

The ones I used were from CapCut’s auto caption templates, and I intentionally chose the template that best complemented my video. I also increased the size and placed it at the centre of the video to make it as visually appealing as possible (see below for the screenshot).

## 5: Short video

Another feature of my 6M+ view short was the fact that it was under 20 seconds. 18 seconds to be exact.

The reason you should make your video as short as necessary if you want to go viral is that the longer your video is, the completion rate goes down. It’s an inverse relationship. Attention spans are very limited, and you don’t want to create content people can’t finish.

## 6: Viral idea itself

Last but not least, the video idea itself had viral potential. This one is key. I had seen another AI based account on Instagram couple months prior with the exact same script which went super viral and got tens of millions of views.

When coming up with content ideas, it’s useful to take inspiration from videos in your niche that performed well. Think 1–5M+ views. This is because demand has already been proven, so rather than trying to come up with an original idea every time, which you assume will be a high performer, take existing high performers and make them your own. This is exactly what I did and gained just under 47k subs because of it.

In your content, if you combine high quality visuals, quick transitions, a tight script and a validated idea, you’ve maximised the chance of your video going viral. Most creators focus on 1 or 2 of these, but the real advantage comes from combining these principles consistently across all your videos, regardless of platform (whether Instagram, TikTok or Shorts).

 By Tinyfool · Published August 18, 2026

I did not choose this case simply because her channel is large. I chose it because YouTube published a direct conversation between Hoyos and Shorts Product Lead Todd Sherman, and Hoyos demonstrated her story structure in a TED talk. We can study her own words instead of reverse-engineering a viral video and pretending we know why it worked.

Evidence checked August 18, 2026 (site time). Her main channel displayed 12.4M subscribers and 4.8K videos. That scale makes her worth studying, but it does not make every preference a universal YouTube rule.
...
I would also ignore any universal retention number attributed to this approach. The first-party sources do not give us one. A benchmark taken from somebody else’s format, audience and channel history can make a perfectly useful video look broken.
...
Hoyos and Sherman make a helpful distinction: inside the Shorts feed, the first frame and first second do much of the choice work because the video is already playing. That makes a thumbnail less important for initial feed discovery than it is for a long-form video.
...
- Did the Short get an opportunity? “Shown in feed” tells you about distribution, not viewer choice.
- Did people choose to continue? “Chose to view” or “Stayed to watch” helps inspect the opening, but no single percentage proves the hook was good.
- Where did the story lose attention? Audience retention lets you inspect the question, progress, obstacle and payoff separately. A dip points to a moment; it does not explain the cause by itself.

Then I would look beyond the single view count. Did the Short produce engaged views under YouTube’s current definition? Did it generate subscribers? Did those viewers return, or continue into another video in the series?

This distinction matters because YouTube changed public Shorts view counting on March 31, 2025: starts and replays now count differently, while “engaged views” remains available in Analytics. Comparisons across measurement eras need care.

The YouTube Analytics Guide explains how to read these signals together. The Get Consistent Views Guide provides the broader diagnosis: opportunity, viewer choice, delivery and the path to another useful video.
...
For a small YouTuber, that idea becomes useful when it turns into a controlled batch and is judged with the channel’s own Analytics. Public success tells us Hoyos is worth listening to. It does not turn her process into a guaranteed ranking formula.
